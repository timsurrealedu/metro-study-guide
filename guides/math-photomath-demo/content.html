Completing the square turns any quadratic into vertex form, exposing both the turning point and the exact x-intercepts on the graph.

Two views of the same parabola

A quadratic equation [[a|c]]x^{2} + [[b|g]]x + [[c|r]] = 0 draws a U-shaped curve. The Quadratic Formula solves for where that curve crosses the x-axis.

x = \frac{-[[b|g]] \pm \sqrt{[[b|g]]^{2} - 4[[a|c]][[c|r]]}}{2[[a|c]]} The expression inside the square root (\Delta = b^{2} - 4ac) decides how many times the parabola hits the x-axis.
Completing the squareAdding (\\frac{b}{2})^{2} to both sides so x^{2} + bx folds neatly into one squared binomial (x + p)^{2}.
DiscriminantThe quantity \Delta = b^{2} - 4ac under the radical that counts real roots before you finish solving.

A composite function wraps an inner rule inside an outer rule. Differentiate the outside layer first, then multiply by the speed of the inside layer.

Peeling outer and inner functions

Think of gears connected together: if the inner gear turns twice as fast as x, the whole machine changes twice as fast too.

\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[[[\sin|f|out]]([[x^{2} + 1|g|in]])\bigr] = [[\cos|f|out]]([[x^{2} + 1|g|in]]) \cdot [[2x|r|du]] Violet = outer function & derivative · Teal = inner expression kept frozen · Green = inner derivative multiplier
Why does the inside stay x² + 1 during the first step?

The outer rate of change depends on the exact input value currently fed into sine, which is still [[x^{2}+1|g]], not 2x.

Composite functionA function nested inside another function, written [[f|f]]([[g(x)|g]]).
Tangent lineA straight line that just grazes the curve at one point x_{0} with slope equal to f'(x_{0}).

A definite integral adds up infinitely many razor-thin rectangles under a curve. Antiderivatives give that exact total in two quick subtractions.

From discrete rectangles to exact area

Approximating area with [[n|c]] rectangles always leaves small gaps. As [[n|c]] \to \infty, the gaps vanish and the sum becomes the definite integral.

\lim_{n \to \infty}\sum_{k=1}^{n} f(x_{k})\,\Delta x = \int_{[[a|c]]}^{[[b|g]]} [[f(x)|f]]\dd{x} = [[F(b)|r]] - [[F(a)|k]] Left: Riemann sum of n boxes · Middle: definite integral from a to b · Right: Fundamental Theorem of Calculus
Riemann sumAdding rectangle areas f(x_{k}) \cdot \Delta x across equal subintervals of width \Delta x = \frac{b-a}{n}.
AntiderivativeA function F(x) whose derivative brings back f(x), such as \frac{x^{3}}{3} for x^{2}.

Subtracting lower limit first
Always compute F(\text{top}) - F(\text{bottom}). Reversing the order flips the sign of the entire integral.
Adding + C to a definite integral without limits
Even if you write + C, subtracting (F(b)+C) - (F(a)+C) cancels C away completely. Definite integrals output a number.

An augmented matrix strips away variable names so row operations solve two lines at once. A zero determinant shows the lines are parallel and never meet at one point.

Augmented matrix and determinant

Each row encodes one straight line in the xy-plane. A nonzero determinant guarantees the two lines cross at one unique point.

\begin{bmatrix} [[2|c]] & [[1|c]] & [[5|g]] \\ [[1|c]] & [[-1|c]] & [[1|g]] \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{and} \quad \begin{vmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{vmatrix} = (2)(-1) - (1)(1) = [[-3|r]] Left: augmented matrix [A | b] · Right: determinant ad − bc ≠ 0 confirms a unique crossing point
Augmented matrixA compact grid packing the coefficient matrix A beside the target constants b.
DeterminantThe scalar ad - bc measuring area scaling; if zero, the two lines never cross at a single point.

Adding columns instead of rows
Elementary row operations combine horizontal rows (equations). Never add vertical columns together in an augmented matrix.
Using + instead of − in ad − bc
The off-diagonal product bc is always subtracted from the main diagonal ad.
